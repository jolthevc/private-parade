# Collection and design playbook

## 1. Develop the collection
Pitch several **genuinely different** collection ideas, not renamed variations. For each, explain: title, one-line premise, emotional tension, audience appeal, distinctive visual opportunity, potential five-piece variety, and risks. Select one based on originality, wearability, coherence and depth. Collections are released **sporadically**, when good enough.

Lock a short collection bible: name/number, premise, emotional register, visual references (study technique, do not imitate living artists' distinctive works), typography strategy, palette logic, motifs, things to avoid, garment-color strategy, and what makes the five pieces belong together. Produce a consumer-facing introduction that is evocative but not overwrought.

## 2. Design five pieces, one at a time
For each piece:
1. Give it a working title and 2–3 materially different concepts/compositions. Describe what a viewer sees from ten feet away.
2. Develop **one** selected direction as isolated artwork. Test silhouette, focal hierarchy, negative space, surprise, linework, intentional detail and visual identity.
3. Critique against the five tests: **desirability, originality, craft, wearability, collection fit**. Be willing to start over.
4. Develop the front: recognizable PRIVATE PARADE lettering, usually small left chest (~3 inches), with collection-appropriate decoration, color, typographic interpretation or symbol. The front is an intentional small design, not a disconnected logo pasted on.
5. Render front/back on the **exact verified** Printify blank and available color; compare alternate graphic scales. Avoid automatic full-back coverage. Respect printable dimensions.
6. Refine until explicitly approved. Preserve final artwork, transparent/print-ready exports, reference mockups and placement measurements.
7. Write consumer-facing title, one-sentence hook, short editorial description, and accurate garment details. No words printed on the artwork unless intentionally approved.
8. Choose eligible garment extensions (crewneck/hoodie) selectively and redesign placement as needed.

## Art direction rules
- A strong subject and clear composition beat a fully populated scene.
- Deliberate omissions and negative space are virtues. Detail belongs where it earns attention.
- Strong, varied line weight and flat shapes may be appropriate; style must be chosen per collection.
- Weathered or tattered-looking artwork is welcome when art-directed: broken illustrated edges, subtle irregularity, and restrained worn-ink feel. Never default to splatter, grunge overlays, fake paper texture, hard frames or stock retro distress.
- A strange element must be visually legible if central to the idea; do not force one into every design.
- Avoid identical compositions across the five pieces. Cohesion is not repetition.
- Use authentic art-history/design references to reason about composition and technique; avoid direct copying.
- Check the final art **without** the mockup, **on** the mockup, and at thumbnail/distance scale.
- Color and typography are **collection-specific**, not universal brand rules.
- Product imagery should show true print scale and approximate garment drape. Validate with samples.

## Release deliverables
A collection overview for the storefront; five individual titles and descriptions; hero and lineup visuals; accurate garment mockups; print masters; verified SKU/color/provider records; pricing and contribution checks; sample sign-off; launch-ready product copy. No obligation to launch on a cadence.

## Review rubric
Score each 1–5: visual impact, distinctive authorship, composition/restraint, print execution, garment fit, collection coherence. Anything below 4 in impact or distinctiveness goes back for work. Numbers guide discussion, not substitute for taste.

## Learning loop
Initially review sales, winning designs, order size and ad creative performance **manually**. Record only durable learnings. Do not build a tracking dashboard prematurely.
